What This Is

Robots are starting to move out of factories and into places like homes, where they'll need to work alongside people rather than repeat one fixed motion forever. A new kind of AI model — trained on huge amounts of robot video and language — promises to make this possible: show it a task in plain English, and it figures out the motion itself, rather than being hand-programmed for every situation.

This project takes one of these models and asks four practical questions: can we teach it to reliably do a real task on a specific robot? Does what it learns on one robot arm carry over to a different one? Can it work alongside a person, asking for help when it gets stuck rather than failing silently? And does any of this actually work on a real physical robot, not just in simulation?

What We Found

The single biggest lesson of this project: when something didn't work, it was almost always traceable to a specific, fixable problem with the data or the setup — not a fundamental limit of the AI model itself.

  • Careful setup matters more than raw training time. Switching how the robot's movements were represented, and fixing a dataset that was silently missing an entire task, made a bigger difference than any amount of extra training.
  • Extending to new robot arms works — up to a point. The AI adapted to two further robot arms individually. Whether training one AI on all three robots at once actually helps (versus training three separate ones) is the one open question this project hasn't answered yet — an early attempt to measure this was invalidated by a bug, and a corrected version is running now.
  • Asking for help works, but simply. We built a system where the robot attempts a task on its own, and after a few failed tries, hands it to a person to finish by hand — then carries on automatically. It's a basic hand-off, not a negotiation, but it works.
  • Simulation-trained skill didn't transfer directly to the real robot — it failed every time. Training a new, smaller policy directly on real demonstrations, instead, worked about half the time. That gap between simulation and reality is the project's most important open problem.
